Why I built this

Reading the news and knowing what it means for you aren't the same thing

For a long time, I'd spend 30 to 45 minutes every day across the New York Times, Reuters, Bloomberg, FT, and CNA, trying to piece together what was actually going on in the world. And most of it still left the same question unanswered: okay, but what does this mean for me, living and working and investing in Singapore?

That's why I built The Daily Ripple. It's an AI-generated daily brief, eight sections, and every story gets tied back to something concrete: your CPF, your HDB mortgage, your SGX holdings, your COE renewal. Not "markets fell today," but why, and what that actually means for someone with a fairly ordinary financial life here. It's not trying to replace the news. More like, read this first, so you're not scrambling to react to every headline that hits your phone.

It started as something I built just for myself. It also turned out to be a good excuse to answer a different question. Could I actually design, build, ship, and run an agentic AI system myself? Not a demo. Something that runs every day on its own, and that I'm on the hook for when it gets something wrong.

Decisions worth explaining

The parts I'd want you to ask me about

Why I stopped routing the brief through Telegraph first

The first version wrote the brief, published it to Telegraph, then had a second AI pass read that back and turn it into the structured data the site needs. Sounded reasonable: human-readable copy, an archive, a clean split between writing and structuring. In practice, Telegraph just doesn't carry everything the site needs (no summary field, no Singapore Lens, no tags), so any time the formatting drifted even slightly, a section would quietly vanish or get mis-tagged. Now the model writes the structured version directly, in one pass. Telegraph's still around, but it's just a nice copy now, not something the pipeline actually depends on.

A strict data shape, loose writing

The site renders 12 fixed fields per story. Every time the shape drifted, something broke or looked off. So the shape of the data became non-negotiable, checked the same way on both the n8n side and the server, and all the flexibility got pushed into the writing instead. A metric's value has to be an actual number, never a sentence. A "Down 20%" once got read as a price and wrecked a chart. Categories are tagged by emoji rather than matching a text label, because I kept renaming the labels in ways a string match couldn't keep up with.

Checking things twice

Every brief gets validated against the same rules twice: once in n8n before it's sent, again on the server before it's saved. Basically, don't trust it just because it came out of my own workflow.

Signals, the part I'm actually excited about

Every brief makes calls, things like "if oil stays above this level, watch this REIT," or "if the Fed holds, expect this on mortgages." Signals tracks those calls over time and shows whether they actually happened. It's less about getting credit for being right, more about holding the brief accountable to itself, since most news commentary predicts things and nobody ever circles back to check. The clean cases, like a price crossing a level, get resolved by plain arithmetic. Everything fuzzier runs through a small set of background agents: one pulls signals out of each brief, one checks them against new evidence, one writes the weekly theme summary, and one puts together the daily "house view," basically the brief's own opinion on the day, with its reasoning attached.

The part that checks a call doesn't just decide on its own

Before resolving anything, it scores how sure it is. Confident enough, it marks the call resolved and shows its evidence. Not sure, it doesn't guess. It puts it in a queue for me to confirm or dismiss myself. I added that after it once marked a Brent oil call as "realised" off a random news snippet that said $126, when my own price feed showed it was actually sitting around $81. If the whole point of a feature is holding itself accountable, it really can't afford to be the thing lying about its own scorecard.

It's made up numbers before, flat out

At one point it reported S&P 500 and Nikkei levels that were off by 25 to 45%. Confident, specific, and wrong. I fixed the two instances by hand at the time. The real fix is catching that before it publishes, which is the evaluation layer I ended up designing after the fact, and the thing I'd build first if I started this over.

What I'd do differently

If I started this over

  • →Build the evaluation layer first, not after. I got it running reliably every day before I built any real way of knowing when it was wrong, which is backwards, especially once more than 55 people are reading it.
  • →Actually log the cost, don't just estimate it. I know roughly what this costs to run, about $18 a month all in, but that's from doing the math by hand, not from an automatic log. Every API call already tells you exactly how many tokens it used. No excuse not to record that automatically.
  • →A "safety" step isn't safe if it loses information. Telegraph felt like a review checkpoint. It was actually a second, undocumented copy of the data shape that quietly drifted out of sync with the real one.
  • →Treat anything the model writes like an outside API response. Check it coming in, check it again at the boundary, and don't assume it's fine just because it's your own pipeline that produced it.

Why I think this matters beyond a newsletter

Same shape as a lot of enterprise AI workflows

Take away the Singapore-newsletter framing and what's left is: pull from multiple sources on a schedule, have an LLM write into a strict format, check that output twice, save it, and push it out, plus a small set of agents that pull out claims, track them against evidence, and know when to hand off to a person instead of guessing. That's the same shape as a research-desk morning note, a compliance summary, or a client-comms generator: the kind of thing an AI platform team inside a bank or exchange is already building internally.

The parts I think actually transfer: treat anything an LLM writes as untrusted input, the same way you'd treat any external API response. Don't let a "human-readable" middle step quietly become a second copy of your schema that nobody's watching. And give an autonomous agent a confidence threshold and an actual handoff to a person, instead of either trusting it blindly or reviewing everything by hand.
